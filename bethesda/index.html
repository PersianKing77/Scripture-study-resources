The Pool of Bethesda
Prepared by Taylor Halverson, Ph.D.

BetaPlans, dates and identifications are still being reviewed. Where scholars disagree — the date of the southern pool, whether the eastern basins were Jewish ritual baths or a healing shrine, how much of a pagan cult existed before AD 70, the exact plan of the Byzantine church — the model follows the reading most excavators have accepted and the essay says so.

What this is

Nine building phases in one hollow of rock, from the Iron Age dam of about 800 BC to the excavated garden you can walk through today. Each phase is a reconstructed model of what actually stood on the ground, drawn in a common set of coordinates so that the phases sit on top of one another exactly as the archaeology does — and each is paired with an essay of roughly a thousand words written to be read straight through rather than consulted.

The point of building it this way is that no photograph of Bethesda can show what the site is. Standing on the walkway above the pools you are looking at nine sites at once, stacked. A model you can turn, fade, ghost against another age, and lay over the present-day aerial can show that; a picture cannot.

Accuracy & limits

The plan is redrawn from published excavation reports: Mauss's clearance of 1863, the École Biblique campaigns of 1957–62 under de Vaux, Rousée and Duprez, and later re-examinations of the excavation records. Horizontal positions are approximate; heights, roofing and vaulting are reconstruction throughout, and anything drawn with a dashed outline is reconstructed or disputed. The northern pool is still largely unexcavated, so its later phases are the most conjectural thing here. The five porticoes of John 5 are not a guess — the foundations of four around the basins and a fifth on the dam were found — but their elevation is.

The aerial overlay is anchored on the Church of Saint Anne and rotated 12.3° east of the plan's north, a figure measured from the orientation of the built grid in the imagery rather than judged by eye. The pools are buried, so nothing in the photograph gives a sharp edge to lock onto; the fit is a surveyed best estimate and is fixed here so every reader sees the same one.

Taylor Halverson is a scholar of scripture and a teacher of learners — an entrepreneurship professor in the BYU Marriott School of Business who spends his working life helping people find and act on the best ideas and tools available to them.

He is the author and editor of more than fifty books and nearly a thousand articles and blog posts, and a builder of scripture study resources: the ScripturePlus app with Scripture Central, whose scripture YouTube channel he co-hosted to more than fifty million views, and BYU's Virtual Scripture Group, whose team spent more than five years reconstructing New Testament Jerusalem in three dimensions. This atlas of Bethesda belongs to that same body of work — tools made so that ordinary readers can see what specialists see.

He leads tours to Israel and the Middle East, China, India, Central America, Europe, and the national parks of the American West, where he unfolds how geology, geography, civilization, and scriptural texts created our beautiful present day.
