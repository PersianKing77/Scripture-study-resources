The Pool of Bethesda
Prepared by Taylor Halverson, Ph.D.

BetaPlans, dates and identifications are still being reviewed. Where scholars disagree — the date of the southern pool, whether the eastern basins were Jewish ritual baths or a healing shrine, how much of a pagan cult existed before AD 70, the exact plan of the Byzantine church — the model follows the reading most excavators have accepted and the essay says so.

What this is

Nine building phases in one hollow of rock, from the Iron Age dam of about 800 BC to the excavated garden you can walk through today. Each phase is a reconstructed model of what actually stood on the ground, drawn in a common set of coordinates so that the phases sit on top of one another exactly as the archaeology does — and each is paired with an essay of roughly a thousand words written to be read straight through rather than consulted.

The point of building it this way is that no photograph of Bethesda can show what the site is. Standing on the walkway above the pools you are looking at nine sites at once, stacked. A model you can turn, fade, ghost against another age, and lay over the present-day aerial can show that; a picture cannot.

Accuracy & limits

The plan is redrawn from published excavation reports: Mauss's clearance from 1863, published 1888; the École Biblique campaigns of 1957–62 under de Vaux, Rousée and Duprez; and later re-examinations of the excavation records. Horizontal positions are approximate, and heights, roofing and vaulting are reconstruction throughout — there is no separate class of "certain" elevation here, so nothing is marked as such. The only dashed lines you will see are the ghosted comparison phase and the ring that marks a feature the essay has just sent you to. The northern pool is still largely unexcavated, so its later phases are the most conjectural thing here. The five porticoes of John 5 are not a guess — the foundations of four around the basins and a fifth on the dam were found — but their elevation is.

Overlay the site today draws the present compound from this atlas's own line-drawn plan, not from a photograph — there is no aerial or satellite imagery in this product.

Sources

A reconstruction, not a survey. Footprints follow published excavation plans — Mauss's clearance from 1863, published 1888; the École Biblique campaigns of 1957–62 under de Vaux, Rousée and Duprez; and later re-examinations; elevations above ground are informed estimates.

Escape closes any panel. On a narrow screen the three columns scroll sideways rather than stacking, so the model keeps its proportions.

The timeline is spaced evenly by phase, not by elapsed years. Left and right arrow keys move through time; press P for presenter mode, T to start the tour.

Overlaying another age

The model can ghost the previous or next building phase behind the current one, or overlay the site as it looks today or in the age of the Gospel — a faint second layer for seeing what changed. This is a background rendering option rather than a control most readers need, so it isn't exposed on the main screen.
