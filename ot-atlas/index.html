The Old Testament & Intertestamental Atlas

Ur to the death of Herod · prepared by Taylor Halverson, Ph.D.

Two thousand years of Old Testament and intertestamental history laid on the land it happened on: 157 places, 53 journeys, 41 military campaigns, 124 events, 80 excavated objects, nineteen ancient peoples and thirteen eras, each drawn in real coordinates and tied to the verse that reports it, with the 52-week reading year mapped onto them.

Beta Places, routes, dates and identifications are still being reviewed. Where scholars disagree — the location of Sinai, the date of the exodus, the sea crossing, the cities of the plain — the atlas follows the traditional identification and says so in the entry rather than choosing silently. For the Exodus, Conquest and Judges dates specifically, use the chronology-model selector next to the timeline at the bottom of the screen to choose the traditional (15th c. BC), archaeological (13th c. BC), or both-shown view.
On the evidence

Every scripture reference in the atlas — more than six hundred across places, events, journeys, campaigns, finds and the 52 weekly readings — was checked against book and chapter counts. Regnal dates follow Thiele’s synchronised chronology. Inscription dates, findspots and museum holdings were checked against published translations and museum records; disputed points — Samaria falling in 722 or 720, the figure on the Black Obelisk — are named as disputed.

On the routes

Every route leg was checked against the coastline and stepped through real roads, rivers and caravan stations: none crosses water, and none runs more than about 200 km without a waystation — except the Arabian incense road, where the desert genuinely is that empty. Routes follow the historic network (Via Maris, the King’s Highway, the Euphrates road) but remain schematic, and kingdom outlines are approximate zones, not surveyed frontiers.

On the Jerusalem plan

Walls, gates, valleys and the water system are drawn from the excavated and surveyed lines in four building phases — David, Solomon, Hezekiah, Nehemiah. Reconstructed stretches are named as such in the entry.

The map is the document; everything else is a way of asking a question about it. Thirteen eras run down the left and across the bottom, layers sit above the map, and the panel on the right holds whatever you last clicked.

Finding your way in
  • Weekly Reading (key w) opens the reading year: pick a week and the map sets itself to that reading — era, layers, places and one note on what the ground adds.
  • Tours is the easiest start: eight narrated walks that set the map up stop by stop.
  • Presenter (key m) strips the controls and enlarges every label for a classroom projector.
  • Scale in the left rail jumps between the four scales the Old Testament is read at — Crescent, Egypt and Sinai, Canaan, the ridge country — or straight into Jerusalem (shift-1 to shift-5).
  • Thirteen eras across the bottom and down the left; arrow keys step through them.
  • Search (key /) reaches places, events, peoples, objects, city features and scripture references.
Layers and lines
  • Layer chips at the top of the map, with Clear, All and Reset; number keys toggle them. Journeys, places and events show by default — campaigns, finds, roads and tribes are opt-in.
  • Hover any line to name it; click a journey or campaign for its stage-by-stage distances and days.
  • Clicking anything in the right panel rings it on the map and turns its layer on for you.
Time and text
  • Timeline (key C) opens the chronology: Judah and Israel’s kings side by side, the prophets who overlap them, and the empires above. Click a reign to read it; click a prophet to fly to his home town.
  • Every scripture reference is a button. In the Verses tab, place names inside the text are clickable and the map follows the verse you are reading.
Seeing the ground
  • Jerusalem plan (key J), or open Jerusalem’s entry and pick a building phase.
Sources
  • Coastlines and modern borders: Natural Earth, public domain, 1:50m.
  • Scripture text: American Standard Version (1901), public domain worldwide — no jurisdiction requires a licence.
  • Relief: NASA shaded relief with bathymetry, public domain, served through NASA GIBS. Imagery needs a connection; without one the atlas falls back to the drawn map.
  • Elevations are the recorded heights of the named places, quoted in each place entry.
  • Dates are editorial choices, not settled facts: each era says which chronology it follows and what the alternative is. In beta these are under review.

The intertestamental centuries

The four eras after Malachi rest on different evidence from the rest of the atlas: 1–2 Maccabees, Josephus, Polybius, the papyri, coins and excavation rather than the Hebrew canon. Each era says which source carries it, and the entries name where a single ancient historian is the only witness.
