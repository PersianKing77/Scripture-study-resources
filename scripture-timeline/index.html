How to read this page

This page pairs two things on purpose: what mainstream historical scholarship says about the Bible's setting, writing, and recognition as scripture, and what Restoration scripture adds to that picture. Toggle "Restoration data" on to see the second layer.

Why three rows per book?

Narrative setting — when the events the book describes take place (e.g. Exodus is set c. 1400 BC). Composition — when it was actually written down, which is often centuries after the events, sometimes in stages. Canonization — when it was formally recognized as scripture by a religious community. Stacking all three lets you see the real gaps between story-time, writing-time, and recognition-time at a glance, instead of collapsing a book into one single date.

Book of Abraham

Not a Bible book, and not a revision of one — the Book of Abraham presents itself as Abraham's own record, translated by Joseph Smith 1835–1842 from papyri and canonized in 1880. It traditionally sits in the patriarchal era, c. 2000–1900 BC, contemporary with the setting of Genesis 12–25, and includes cosmology and creation material distinct from, though related to, Genesis 1. There is no mainstream critical composition date to compare it against — it stands as its own record.
